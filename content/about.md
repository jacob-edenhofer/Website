I am a DPhil candidate in Politics at the University of Oxford, supervised by [Federica Genovese](https://federica-genovese.com/) and [David Rueda](https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/people/profiles/david-rueda/). I aim to become a comparative political economist whose principal applied research interest is climate politics. My research examines when temporary electoral pressure for climate action can produce lasting increases in policy stringency. Governments choose policies while anticipating whether public attention will persist and how voters will respond as costs become visible. Firms decide whether to invest in cleaner production partly according to whether they expect those policies to continue. I examine when early investment reduces subsequent costs of compliance, weakens organised opposition, and helps sustain stringent policy after the initial electoral pressure subsides. Delayed adjustment may leave policy vulnerable to dilution or reversal. This offers a brief preview of the argument developed in my DPhil manuscript, *Uncertain Mandates, Durable Stringency*. Related work examines whether compensation can sustain public support when governments have limited information about losses, and whether institutions and bargaining with producers encourage adjustment or protect established interests. Other projects investigate how governments develop the capacity to tax, how economic decline contributes to populism, how inflation affects preferences for redistribution, and how technocracy relates to democratic accountability.

In working on these projects, I am often torn, as a young scholar, between the lofty ideals of modern social science and the realities of what my supervisor Federica Genovese calls an ‘incentivised craft’. For me, those ideals centre on a reflective equilibrium between theory and evidence, in which theoretical arguments guide empirical inquiry and empirical findings prompt us to reconsider our assumptions, explanations, and questions ([Ashworth et al., 2021](https://academic.oup.com/princeton-scholarship-online/book/42453)). This requires us to scrutinise our research designs and understand when findings from studies conducted under different circumstances permit meaningful comparisons ([Blair et al., 2023](https://book.declaredesign.org/); [Izzo et al., 2025](https://www.federicaizzo.com/pdf/ssrn-3239047.pdf)). Such progress requires a willingness to be usefully wrong, although being right is welcome too—a sentiment reflected in the title of my Substack, [*Often wrong, but sometimes useful*](https://jacobedenhofer.substack.com/). The pressure to publish can encourage us to retrofit questions and theory to familiar methods or promising results and to leave less convenient findings unpublished. I try to resist these temptations as best I can, knowing that I will manage this tension more successfully at some times than at others. I include the [file drawer](/file-drawer/) to document unsuccessful projects and make their assumptions, limitations, and mistakes available for criticism. This website is therefore both a statement of purpose and a commitment device through which I hope to hold myself accountable to these ideals.
