I am a DPhil candidate in Politics at the University of Oxford, supervised by [Federica Genovese](https://federica-genovese.com/) and [David Rueda](https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/people/profiles/david-rueda/) (both University of Oxford). I am a comparative political economist whose principal applied interest is climate policy in rich democracies. My research examines how electoral incentives and organised interest groups interact when democratic governments address long-term policymaking problems, which involve accepting costs today for potential benefits in the future ([Lindvall, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0951629810369524), [2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198766865.001.0001); [Jacobs, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511921766), [2016](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-110813-034103)). I also examine how information and state capacity affect policy design, implementation, and durability. Related projects examine how citizens respond to economic losses, how governments acquire the capacity to target fiscal interventions, and how institutions allocate influence between voters, organised interests, and experts. As a young scholar, I am still developing my research profile and working out how best to balance the research in which I have a comparative advantage with the work most likely to contribute to the accumulation of knowledge.

Ideally, modern social science should be an institutionalised reflective equilibrium between theory and empirical work—through which arguments guide empirical inquiry and findings prompt revisions to our assumptions, explanations, and questions ([Ashworth et al., 2021](https://academic.oup.com/princeton-scholarship-online/book/42453); [Little and Pepinsky, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1086/710088))—with each scholar contributing according to their comparative advantage. In addition to clear theorising and, as the credibility revolution has taught us, attention to the identification challenges associated with testing the empirical implications of theories, this also requires care in thinking about the scope conditions and external validity of findings ([Gailmard, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1561/115.00000003); [Blair et al., 2023](https://book.declaredesign.org/); [Slough and Tyson, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12742), [2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009375856); [Izzo et al., 2025](https://www.federicaizzo.com/pdf/ssrn-3239047.pdf); [Findley et al., 2026](https://externalvalidity.com/book/)).

More fundamentally, perhaps, this requires the willingness by individual researchers to be usefully wrong (though being right never hurts of course)—a sentiment reflected in the title of my Substack, [*Often wrong, but sometimes useful*](https://jacobedenhofer.substack.com/). Yet the incentives of academic publishing and career progression can encourage researchers to retrofit questions to convenient methods or promising results and leave unsuccessful projects unpublished. In working on these projects, I try to resist these temptations, knowing that I will sometimes fall short. Critical scrutiny of the incentives of how we do social science is and suggestions for reform are much needed, particularly in the age of generative artificial intelligence ([Munger, 2026](https://kevinmunger.substack.com/p/peer-review-2027)). But a single-minded focus on what is wrong risks giving rise to the impression that the entire system is broken.

My own experience gives me reasons for a more hopeful view. In many ways, this website is an homage to the generous senior academics who have mentored me, from my supervisors to my co-authors and beyond, and to the ideals of intellectual curiosity, rigour, and generosity that they embody. It contains my [research](/research/), [public engagement and writing](/writing/), [teaching materials](/teaching/), [service](/service/), [CV](/Jacob_Edenhofer_CV.pdf), and contact details. In the [file drawer](/file-drawer/), I collect unsuccessful ideas and projects in the hope that others can learn from them. Finally, the website also serves as a commitment device through which I hope to hold myself accountable to the ideals required to do good (or at any rate usefully wrong) social science research.
