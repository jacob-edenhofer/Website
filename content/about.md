I am a DPhil candidate in Politics at the University of Oxford, supervised by [Federica Genovese](https://federica-genovese.com/) and [David Rueda](https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/people/profiles/david-rueda/) (both University of Oxford). I study how electoral incentives and organised interest groups interact when democratic governments address problems that require sustained action. Climate politics is my principal applied interest. I am also interested in how information and state capacity affect the policies governments can design, implement, and sustain. Related projects examine how citizens respond to economic losses, how governments acquire the capacity to target fiscal interventions, and how institutions allocate influence between voters, organised interests, and experts. As a young scholar, I am still developing a research profile within comparative political economy and working out where my comparative advantage might help me contribute usefully to the accumulation of knowledge.

Ideally, modern social science should be an institutionalised reflective equilibrium between theory and empirical work, with each scholar contributing according to their comparative advantage. Arguments guide empirical inquiry, and findings prompt revisions to our assumptions, explanations, and questions ([Ashworth et al., 2021](https://academic.oup.com/princeton-scholarship-online/book/42453)). This requires careful research design and attention to the accumulation of knowledge across circumstances ([Blair et al., 2023](https://book.declaredesign.org/); [Slough and Tyson, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12742), [2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009375856); [Izzo et al., 2025](https://www.federicaizzo.com/pdf/ssrn-3239047.pdf); [Findley et al., 2026](https://externalvalidity.com/book/)). Progress requires a willingness to be usefully wrong, although being right is welcome too—a sentiment reflected in my Substack, [*Often wrong, but sometimes useful*](https://jacobedenhofer.substack.com/). Yet the incentives of academic publishing can encourage researchers to retrofit questions to convenient methods or promising results and leave unsuccessful projects unpublished. In working on these projects, I try to resist these temptations, knowing that I will sometimes fall short.

Criticism of the incentives under which we practise social science is warranted, and reform is much needed. Yet such criticism can give the impression that the entire system is broken. My own experience gives me reasons for a more hopeful view. In many ways, this website is an homage to the generous senior academics who have mentored me, from my supervisors to my co-authors and beyond. It contains my [research](/research/), [writing for wider audiences](/writing/), [teaching materials](/teaching/), [service](/service/), [CV](/Jacob_Edenhofer_CV.pdf), and contact details. Its [file drawer](/file-drawer/) shares unsuccessful ideas and projects in the hope that others can learn from them. The website also serves as a commitment device through which I hope to hold myself accountable to the ideals of modern social science and the example my mentors have provided.
